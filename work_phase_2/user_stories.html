Efficiency-Focused Office Worker

Ying Zhou, 28, currently lives in Melbourne CBD and works as a data analyst. She leads a fast-paced life and values efficiency, switching easily between Chinese and English. She often cooks meals that can serve both as dinner and the next day's lunch. She usually searches and saves recipes on her phone but is often frustrated by lengthy steps, unclear preparation processes, and impractical ingredient portions. She hopes a recipe website can offer reverse search based on what's in her fridge, a 20-minute cooking plan, auto-generated shopping lists, and weekly templates that reuse ingredients, with clear labels for time, steps, and required tools. When deciding if a recipe suits her, she looks mainly at total cooking time, number of steps, availability of common utensils, and real feedback from other users.

How do you usually sort out dinner after work? Why?

After a full day at the office, I don't want to spend too much time in the kitchen. Most nights I'll either pick up some semi-prepped ingredients and veggies from the supermarket and throw something together in 15-20 minutes, or just order takeaway. But I keep takeaway to maybe twice a week — I still want to eat reasonably healthy and not waste too much money.

As a busy worker, I want a quick-meal filter and clear 20-minute cooking plans, so that I can decide what to cook after work without wasting time.

What are the top three problems you'd like a recipe website to solve for you?

For me, it'd be: Helping me quickly answer "What can I cook tonight in under 20 minutes?" Letting me search recipes based on what's already in my fridge. Giving me meal pairings so I can cook once and cover dinner plus tomorrow's lunch.

As a busy worker, I want to search recipes based on the ingredients already in my fridge, so that I can avoid extra shopping and reduce food waste.

What's the biggest barrier for you when cooking at home?

Honestly, it's when the steps are way too long and the prep isn't clear. I'm never sure what I can do at the same time, so I waste a lot of minutes. Also, you can't easily buy small portions of ingredients — I end up with big packs that go off before I can finish them.

As a busy worker, I want recipe suggestions that cover both dinner and tomorrow's lunch, so that I can cook once and save effort for the next day.

What time-saving features would you love to see on a recipe site?

I'd love a one-click “20-minute game plan” that shows which steps I can do in parallel. An auto shopping list would be handy too, ideally with options for brands and pack sizes. And a weekly five-meal template that reuses ingredients across the week would save me heaps of time and money.

As a busy worker, I want an auto-generated shopping list with options for brands and pack sizes, so that I can shop faster and more accurately.

How do you decide if a recipe is right for you?

I usually check a few things: prep and cook time under 30 minutes, no more than six steps, only common kitchen gear, and most importantly, whether people in the comments say they really pulled it off in 20-30 minutes. If all that lines up, I'm keen to give it a go.

As a busy worker, I want weekly meal templates that reuse ingredients, so that I can save time, money, and keep a healthier routine.

Busy Parent

Chen Mei is 35 and lives in Brisbane with her partner and a five-year-old son. She works part-time in HR and manages most weeknight dinners. Time and nutrition matter most: meals must be mild, balanced and ready in under 30 minutes. She switches between Chinese and English and usually shops at Coles plus the local Asian grocer. She's looking for a recipe website that filters for kid-friendly dishes, clear step-by-step photos, and lunchbox ideas she can batch-cook.

What types of dishes do you usually cook for your child and family? Why?

Most weeknights I just knock up something simple and mild — like a quick stir-fry with chicken and veg, or a noodle soup. Nothing fancy. It's easy, the little one will actually eat it, and it keeps everyone happy.

As a busy parent, I want recipesfor mild, balanced family meals, so that both my child and adults can enjoy dinner together.

When you browse recipes, what information matters most to you?

Honestly, I need really clear step-by-step photos — like, show me exactly what to do. And I always check if it's balanced: enough protein, some veggies. But the big one is whether it says it's kid-friendly, otherwise I just skip it.

As a busy parent, I want recipes with clear step-by-step photos and nutrition info, so that I can cook confidently and know it's good for my child.

What obstacles have made you give up trying new recipes?

Oh, plenty. If a recipe has too many steps or asks for random ingredients I can't even find at Coles, I just close the page. And if I see lots of little fiddly prep, I'm like, nope — not happening on a weeknight.

As a busy parent, I want recipes with simple steps and easy-to-find ingredients, so that I don't give up before even starting.

If the website could help you filter recipes quickly, what filters would you want?

I'd love filters like "kid-friendly", "under 30 minutes", "lots of veggies", and maybe "good for lunchboxes". That way I don't waste time scrolling through stuff that doesn't fit my family.

As a busy parent, I want recipe filters like kid-friendly, under 30 minutes, high in veggies, and lunchbox-suitables, so that I can quickly find what fits my family.

How do you judge whether a recipe is 'healthy' or 'kid-friendly'?

I look at the ingredients first — are they fresh? Then I check if there's a good balance of veggies and protein. If it's super oily, deep-fried, or too spicy, I just know my son won't touch it, so that's a no.

As a busy parent, I want recipes that use fresh ingredients, balanced veggies and protein, and mild flavours, so that I can trust they're healthy and kid-friendly.

Visually impaired user

Wang Shun is 68, a retired teacher living in Sydney's Hurstville. He has low vision and uses an Android phone with a screen reader and large-text settings. He values safety and clarity over variety. He's seeking a recipe site with high-contrast themes, one-tap font magnification, alt text for images, audio step guides, and printable large-type PDFs with clear timers and utensil lists.

How do you usually get recipe information?

Most of the time, I just let my screen reader read through text-based recipes. It works well if the structure is clean, but if the text is messy, it gets confusing fast. Sometimes I ask my family to record the main steps as short voice notes, so I can replay them while I'm cooking. I also like printed large-text versions — they're much easier on my eyes, especially when I don't feel like listening to my phone the whole time. Having a few different options really helps me stay independent.

As a visually impaired user, I want recipes in formats that work with my screen reader, voice notes, and large print, so that I can follow them in whichever way is most comfortable and safe for me.

What's the biggest barrier when using recipe websites?

The biggest issue is poor contrast and small text that won't zoom in properly. Sometimes I have to strain my eyes just to read the titles. Another big one is images without alt text — the screen reader just says "image" and skips it, which means I miss key details. And if a site doesn't structure the steps properly, the screen reader dumps it all out in one big block. That's just impossible to follow when you're in the kitchen.

As a visually impaired user, I want high contrast, scalable text, alt text on images, and properly structured steps, so that I can access all the information without strain or confusion.

What accessibility features would you value most?

A one-tap large-text mode with a high-contrast theme would make a huge difference. Every single image needs proper alt text, not just “image.” The whole site should be easy to use with a screen reader, with clear headings and links that I can skip through on the keyboard. And if there were downloadable audio versions of each recipe, that'd be fantastic — I could listen hands-free while cooking. Printable large-text PDFs are also really useful for when I want to cook without relying on the phone at all.

As a visually impaired user, I want one-tap large text, high-contrast themes, alt text, audio guides, and printable PDFs, so that I can cook more easily and confidently at home.

Which format is easiest for you to follow?

Numbered steps are the clearest — and they need to be short, no more than two sentences each. I also like it when each step starts with a label like “prep,” “cook,” or “note,” so I know what kind of action I'm about to take. For ingredients, a table is best — with the name, the amount, and possible substitutes. That way I can quickly get the info I need without the screen reader jumping all over the place.

As a visually impaired user, I want short numbered steps with clear labels and ingredient tables, so that I can process the recipe efficiently and without confusion.

How do you check if a recipe is reliable and doable?

I usually start by checking the source — if it's from a well-known author, organisation, or site I trust, I feel safer trying it. Then I see if the steps actually work with the screen reader. If it skips or messes things up, I don't bother. I also pay attention to the comments — if other people say they've made it successfully, that's a big green light. And if I can download or print a large-text PDF with clear timers and instructions, I know I can actually use it in the kitchen.
